OPINION
Abstain Won the Conversation, but Who Decides What Happens Next?
The Cloud Sentinel
Published Nov 20, 2026 01:34:00 PM PHT

STO TOMAS, BATANGAS | The results of the November 6, 2026, Central Student Council (CSC) elections at the Polytechnic University of the Philippines Sto. Tomas Campus should have given students a clear answer to one question: who will lead their student government for the next academic year?

Instead, the presidential race has left the campus with another question: what happens when the students' votes do not produce a winner under the rules governing the election?

The Commission on Elections (COMELEC), through Resolution No. 004, Series of 2026, adopted a presidential conclave as the official course of action for selecting the next CSC president. The decision followed a contested election in which neither presidential candidate obtained the required 50% threshold, according to the Commission's interpretation of the Electoral Code.

But beyond the decision itself, the situation exposes a more fundamental problem: the Electoral Code does not clearly explain what should happen when a contested presidential election fails to produce a candidate who meets the required threshold.

That gap should concern every student, not only because it has delayed the selection of the next CSC president, but because the process now being introduced will shape the leadership of the campus's highest student governing body.

When abstain nearly matched the leading candidate

The initial and unofficial tally released on November 7 at 8:30 p.m. recorded 2,020 valid ballots for the presidential race:

  • Christopher R. Gutierrez - 715 votes
  • Abstain - 699 votes
  • Kent Simon C. Escobar - 606 votes

Abstain did not win the election. But with only 16 votes separating it from the leading candidate, its tally was difficult to dismiss as insignificant.

Gutierrez received approximately 35.4% of all valid ballots, while Escobar received 30%. Abstain accounted for approximately 34.6%, nearly matching the leading candidate and exceeding the other candidate's tally.

This raises an important question: why is abstain an option in the first place, and what does a vote for it actually mean?

Under the PUP Sto. Tomas Electoral Code, abstention is defined as a voter's decision not to select any candidate. It is not an affirmative vote against either candidate. In other words, students who selected abstain did not necessarily endorse one candidate over the other, nor did they formally vote to disqualify both.

Nevertheless, the size of the abstention vote deserves attention. Whether it reflected dissatisfaction with the choices, uncertainty about the candidates, or another reason cannot be established by the tally alone. What it does show is that a substantial portion of participating students chose neither candidate.

Abstain, therefore, may not have won the presidency, but it certainly became part of the election's central conversation.

Is abstain even an option in national elections?

The comparison with national elections is worth making.

In Philippine national elections, voters are not generally given a separate “ABSTAIN” option on the ballot. A voter may leave a particular position blank while voting for other positions. This is commonly described as an undervote or an item abstention.

The distinction matters. A blank vote does not become a vote against any candidate. It simply means that no candidate was selected for that position.

The more important difference, however, concerns how winners are determined.

Under Article VII, Section 4 of the 1987 Philippine Constitution, the President is elected by obtaining the highest number of votes. The provision does not require a candidate to secure more than 50% of all votes cast to win. The national presidential election follows a plurality system: the candidate with the highest number of votes wins.

The PUP Sto. Tomas student elections, on the other hand, are governed by their own Electoral Code. A 50% threshold may be established for student-government positions, and the Commission is expected to implement that rule.

The point is not that the campus must copy the national electoral system. Student organizations have the authority to establish election rules appropriate to their governance structures. But when those rules differ from the national system, students deserve a clear explanation of why they exist, how they are computed, and what happens when the threshold is not met.

The problem is not simply the 50% threshold. It is the vague Electoral Code.

The 50% requirement is not necessarily the problem by itself. Requiring a candidate to obtain majority support can be a legitimate way to ensure that a student leader has a broad mandate.

The problem is that the Electoral Code appears to leave a critical situation insufficiently addressed.

COMELEC's Resolution No. 004 acknowledges that the Code does not sufficiently establish the procedure for a contested presidential election in which neither candidate obtains the prescribed percentage. It also points out that Section 53.1 expressly provides a 50% requirement for positions with only one running candidate, but does not expressly prescribe a corresponding procedure for determining the winner of a contested presidential race under the circumstances encountered.

That is a serious gap in the rules.

An electoral code should not merely tell students how to vote and how votes are counted. It must also establish what happens when the results do not meet the conditions for declaring a winner. The absence of a clear procedure leaves too much room for interpretation precisely when the stakes are highest.

The resolution itself presents two possible interpretations: declare the candidate with the highest number of votes the winner through plurality, or adopt a presidential conclave as an alternative mechanism.

Both positions were supported by arguments from commissioners. Those favoring plurality maintained that the votes cast should be recognized, that the absence of an absolute majority does not automatically mean the election has failed, and that abstention should not be treated as a vote against either candidate. Those favoring a conclave argued that the absence of a majority, alongside the high abstention tally, warranted a process intended to establish broader support.

But why should these alternatives have to be debated only after the votes have been cast?

The Electoral Code should have anticipated this situation. It should have specified whether the 50% threshold is computed against all valid ballots or only votes cast for candidates, how abstentions are treated in that computation, and what procedure follows if no candidate reaches the required percentage.

The distinction is not trivial. If the denominator is all 2,020 valid ballots, neither candidate reached 50%. If only the 1,321 votes cast for candidates are considered, Gutierrez received approximately 54.1% of candidate votes. These are two different calculations that produce two different conclusions.

The resolution's explanation does not remove the need for the Code to state the applicable basis clearly. Students should not have to guess which interpretation governs their election.

A rule that can determine whether a candidate is declared president or whether an entirely different selection process is initiated must be precise before election day, not interpreted after the results become controversial.

A 50% threshold that needs a clearer definition

There is another question the Electoral Code must answer: 50% of what?

The presidential tally illustrates why this matters. Out of 2,020 valid ballots, Gutierrez received 715 votes, Escobar received 606, and 699 were marked abstain.

If the threshold is computed against all valid ballots, neither candidate reached 50%. But if the computation considers only the 1,321 votes cast for the two candidates, Gutierrez received approximately 54.1%.

These figures do not establish which computation the Electoral Code requires. Rather, they highlight why the Code must explicitly define the denominator and explain how abstentions are treated when applying the threshold.

The Commission's resolution states that abstention is not an affirmative vote against either candidate. However, that does not automatically settle whether abstentions should be included in the computation of the 50% threshold. Those are separate questions, and the Code should answer both.

Without a clear definition, students are left to interpret a rule that directly affects the outcome of their election. The Commission, meanwhile, is left to make a consequential decision using provisions that do not fully address the situation.

This is not merely a technical drafting issue. It is an issue of electoral fairness and institutional accountability.

The Commission chose the conclave. The burden falls on the SLCDs.

The decision to adopt a presidential conclave also raises questions about representation and responsibility.

According to the resolution, the consultative votes of the SLCD Presidents and incumbent CSC President Edwil Grae Zapata favored plurality voting, with 11 votes, compared with 8 for the conclave.

Yet the COMELEC En Banc ultimately voted 13-2 in favor of adopting the conclave.

These were two separate votes, serving different purposes. The consultative result reflected the preference expressed by the SLCD Presidents and the incumbent CSC President; the En Banc vote determined the Commission's official course of action. The consultative vote was not a formal veto, but it is still significant that the mechanism ultimately adopted was not the option favored by the majority of those consulted.

Now, the responsibility of carrying out the process will fall heavily on the Student Leaders of the Different Colleges (SLDCs). The resolution identifies their presidents as the proposed pool of prospective candidates for the CSC presidency, subject to screening, evaluation, eligibility verification, and willingness to serve. The conclave is intended to determine who will lead the campus-wide student government.

In effect, the college organization presidents will be central to a process they did not collectively favor in the consultative vote.

That raises questions that COMELEC must address before the conclave proceeds.

Are the SLCD Presidents prepared to participate in a process whose adoption they did not collectively support? How will the voting body be composed? What threshold will determine the selected president? Will the process require a majority, a plurality, or another standard? How will disagreements and repeated inconclusive ballots be handled?

The resolution leaves several of these operational details for subsequent guidelines. These must be made public before the process begins. Students should know who will participate, how the selection will be conducted, and what safeguards will ensure that the conclave is transparent and accountable.

There is also a practical concern that should not be overlooked: what happens to the college organizations if one of their presidents is selected as CSC president?

Will the organization automatically have a successor? Will its vice president assume the position, or will a special election be necessary? Who will oversee ongoing projects, commitments, and responsibilities during the transition?

The resolution does not expressly settle these questions. The selection of a CSC president should not leave the college organization they currently lead without a clear and orderly succession plan.

The conclave may be intended to produce a leader with broader support, but it must not come at the expense of continuity and representation at the college level.

Students deserve rules that are clear before they vote

The CSC is the highest form of student governance at PUP Sto. Tomas. Its president represents the campus in ANAK PUP, the alliance of student councils across the PUP System. The position carries responsibilities beyond the campus itself.

That is precisely why the selection of its next president must be guided by rules that are clear, consistent, and established before the election, not improvised in response to an unexpected result.

The COMELEC has made its decision through Resolution No. 004. It has chosen the conclave over plurality voting for the present proceedings. That decision now needs to be implemented with clear guidelines, public accountability, and respect for the students who participated in the election.

But the larger issue should not end with the selection of the next president.

The Electoral Code must be reviewed and strengthened. It should clearly define how the 50% threshold is calculated, how abstentions are treated, what happens when no candidate reaches the required percentage, and whether a plurality system, a runoff election, or another mechanism should apply. It should also establish the procedures and safeguards for any alternative selection process.

These provisions must be written into the rules before the next election. Students should not have to wait for a controversy to discover what their Electoral Code failed to explain.

The 699 students who selected abstain deserve to have their choice understood correctly. The candidates deserve a process governed by rules that were clear from the beginning. The SLCDs deserve to know the responsibilities and consequences of the conclave. And the student body deserves to understand how its next campus leader will be chosen.

Abstain did not win the presidency. But its nearly equal tally with the leading candidate exposed questions that the Electoral Code, and the Commission, must confront.

An election is only as credible as the rules governing it. And those rules must be clear before the students cast their votes, not after.
